Yesterday a lot of people ran their first race. If that was you, today is the best day to decide what's next, while you still remember how good the finish felt and before December gets busy.

The obvious next step is a **10K**. It's twice the distance, but it isn't twice as hard to train for: if you can run a 5K, or 30 minutes without stopping, eight weeks is enough. Start on Monday 30 November and you'll be ready for a 10K on the last weekend of January, with Christmas falling on the easiest week of the plan.

## Are you ready for a 10K plan?

One test: **can you run 30 minutes without stopping, at a pace where you could still talk?** If yes, start this plan. If you can run 10–20 minutes, spend a few more weeks building up first (more on that [below](#not-quite-there-yet)). If you ran yesterday's Turkey Trot with walk breaks, that's fine too, it just means a different starting point.

You don't need to be fast. The plan is about time on your feet, not pace. Most first-time 10K runners finish somewhere between 55 and 75 minutes.

## How the eight weeks work

The plan has **four runs a week**: two easy runs, one "quality" session and one long run. About 70% of the running is easy, which is the part most people get wrong: they run every run at the same medium-hard effort and wonder why they're tired.

| Week | Phase | Quality session | Long run |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base | Easy 28 min + 4 × 20 s strides | 45 min |
| 2 | Base | Fartlek: 8 × (1 min fast, 1 min jog) | 55 min, your first 8K |
| 3 | Base | Tempo: 2 × 8 min, 3 min jog between | 60 min |
| 4 | Build | Short hills: 6 × 45 s, walk down | 43 min (cutback) |
| 5 | Build | Tempo: 2 × 10 min | 65 min |
| 6 | Build | Tempo: 20 min continuous | 72 min, about 10.5 km |
| 7 | Peak | 3 × 5 min at 10K effort, 2 min jog | 75 min, last 10 at 10K effort |
| 8 | Race week | Sharpener: 2 × 4 min tempo | **Race: 10K** |

The two easy runs are shorter than the long run and grow slowly with it. Put the long run on the weekend and keep a rest day after the quality session.

A few things worth noticing:

- **You'll run longer than 10K before race day.** By week 6 your long run takes about 72 minutes, roughly 10.5 km at an easy pace. On race day the distance won't be the scary part.
- **Week 4 is deliberately easy.** Every plan needs a lighter week to absorb the work. It's the week your legs catch up.
- **Week 6 has the big one:** 20 minutes of continuous tempo. That's the session that tells you you're ready.

## How hard is "easy"? How hard is "tempo"?

The plan doesn't give paces, because a first 10K should be run by feel. It uses heart rate zones instead, and each one has a feel you can check without a watch.

| Effort | Heart rate | How it feels |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy** (easy runs, long runs) | Zone 2 | You could chat in full sentences |
| **Tempo**, "comfortably hard" | Zone 4 | A few words at a time; you'd rather not talk |
| **10K effort** (week 7) | Zone 4 | Hard but steady, a pace you could hold for an hour |
| **Race day** | Zone 3 at first | Start controlled, then let it build |

Zone 2 is 60–70% of your maximum heart rate. For a 40-year-old woman that's roughly 103–120 bpm, for a 40-year-old man 108–126 bpm. If that feels very slow, it's working. Most new runners are surprised by how slow easy is supposed to be, and [why your heart rate is high when you run slowly]({% post_url 2026-09-25-why-heart-rate-high-running-slow %}) explains why that's normal for the first few months.

**Strides** are 20-second relaxed accelerations to about 90% of your top speed, with a full recovery walk between. They're not sprints. They teach your legs to move quickly without making you tired.

**Fartlek** means "speed play" in Swedish: one minute faster, one minute jogging, no exact pace. It's the gentlest way to introduce faster running.

If you start on **Monday 30 November 2026**:

| 3 | 14 – 20 Dec | |

| 5 | 28 Dec – 3 Jan | New Year's Day falls on Friday |

| 7 | 11 – 17 Jan | Peak week |

The cutback landing on Christmas week is a happy accident. Week 4 is the shortest of the plan: a 43-minute long run and a short hill session. If you miss a run over the holidays, that's the week to miss it.

New Year's Day is a nice spot for the week 5 long run if you're up early. Plenty of towns have a New Year's Day 5K or 10K, so that could be a tune-up too.

Pick your race now and enter it. A 10K on the calendar is what gets you out the door on a cold Tuesday in January.

## Five ways people get the first 10K wrong

1. **Running the easy runs too fast.** If you can't talk, slow down. Easy runs build the engine; tempo runs sharpen it. Running every run medium-hard does neither well.
2. **Racing the tempo sessions.** "Comfortably hard" means you finish thinking you could have done another five minutes. If you're wrecked, it was too fast.
3. **Skipping the long run when it's cold.** The long run matters most. Move it to Saturday or Sunday, whichever has better weather, but don't drop it.
4. **Adding miles in week 4.** The cutback week feels too easy. That's the point.
5. **Going out too fast on race day.** Run the first 2 km slower than feels right. A 10K is long enough to punish a fast start and short enough that you won't notice you're doing it.

## Not quite there yet?

If 30 minutes of running isn't there yet, build up first:

- **You run-walked the Turkey Trot.** A couch-to-5K-style plan takes you from run-walk to 30 minutes of continuous running. In iRunning that's the **0 → 5K** plan: nine weeks, three runs a week, with 15 minutes nonstop in week 5 and 20 in week 6.
- **You walked it.** Start with the free **From Walk to Run** plan, which takes you from walking to five-minute runs in six weeks.

Either way, add the First 10K plan when you finish. There's no rush: 10Ks are everywhere in spring.
