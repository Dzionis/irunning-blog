Some workouts need a track, a stopwatch and a coach's handwriting. The **10-20-30** needs one minute you can count to, and it fits in a lunch break. Searches for it have grown several times over in the past year, mostly by word of mouth: it's short, it's hard in a fun way, and it works.

Here's what it is, why it works, and how to start it in December when your weeks are already full.

## The workout in one minute

One **cycle** is one minute:

| Part | Time | Effort | How it feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Easy** | 30 seconds | Jog | Breathe down; slow enough to recover |
| **Moderate** | 20 seconds | About 10K race effort | Steady, controlled, quicker than easy |
| **Fast** | 10 seconds | Near maximum, but relaxed | A fast stride, not an all-out sprint |

Five cycles in a row make a **5-minute block**. Then jog or walk for **2 minutes**, and do it again. A session is 2 to 4 blocks:

| Blocks | Hard running | Session with warm-up and cool-down |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 min | about 30 min |
| 3 | 15 min (with 4 min of rest) | about 35 min |
| 4 | 20 min (with 6 min of rest) | about 40 min |

That's the whole thing. "12 minutes" in the title is the two-block version: two 5-minute blocks with two minutes between.

## Where it comes from

The 10-20-30 was developed at the University of Copenhagen by Jens Bangsbo's group. In the first study, a group of recreational runners swapped part of their usual running for 10-20-30 sessions for seven weeks, running less in total, and still got faster at 1500 m and 5K.

A bigger follow-up in 2015 (Gliemann and colleagues) took 160 recreational runners from Danish running clubs and gave half of them 10-20-30 sessions for eight weeks. That group improved their 5K by about **38 seconds** on average, while the runners who kept their normal training didn't change much. Blood pressure dropped too, in the runners whose blood pressure had been high to begin with.

Thirty-eight seconds is a lot for eight weeks, and these weren't beginners: they were club runners who'd been training for years.

## Why it works

The clever part is the structure inside each minute:

- **The 10-second sprint** is long enough to recruit your fast-twitch fibres and push your heart rate up, but too short to build up much lactate. You can do it again a minute later.
- **The 30 seconds of jogging** lets you partly recover, so each sprint can be properly fast.
- **The 20 seconds of moderate running** keeps your heart rate high between sprints, so the whole block works your aerobic system hard. Your heart rate climbs through the five cycles and stays up.

Compared to classic intervals like 5 × 1 km, it's easier to start and harder to get wrong. You don't need to know any paces. Easy, steady and fast are enough.

## How hard should the fast part be?

**Fast, not flat-out.** Think 90% of your top speed, relaxed shoulders, quick feet. If your form falls apart in the last two seconds, you went too hard. The last sprint of the block should look like the first one.

And the jog should really be a jog. If the 30 seconds isn't enough to get your breath back by the third block, slow the jog down rather than skipping the sprint.

## How to fit it into your week

**If you run two or three times a week,** make one of those runs a 10-20-30. Start with two blocks and add one every couple of weeks.

**If you run four or more times a week,** it can replace your usual interval session. Keep the other runs easy, as always. Our [easy pace chart]({% post_url 2026-12-05-easy-run-pace-chart %}) shows how easy.

**Over the holidays,** two blocks is the perfect run for a day when you have 30 minutes and a plate of leftovers to work off. It's hard enough to count, short enough to fit.

**Don't do it two days in a row.** It's a hard session, even if it's short.

A rough six-week progression:

| Weeks | Sessions per week | Blocks |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 1–2 | 2 |
| 3–4 | 2 | 3 |
| 5–6 | 2–3 | 3–4 |

Run a 5K time trial in week 1 and again at the end of week 6 to see what changed.

## Building it in iRunning

The 10-20-30 is easy to build yourself in the workout builder:

1. Add a 10-minute **warm-up**.
2. Tap **Add repeat**, then add three intervals inside the group: 30 seconds easy, 20 seconds moderate and 10 seconds fast (time goals go down to 5 seconds). Set the repeat to **5**.
3. Add a single 2-minute jog.
4. Add a second repeat group with the same three intervals × 5 (for three blocks, add another jog and a third group).
5. Add a 10-minute **cool-down**, and save.

Voice cues and a beep countdown tell you when each part starts, so you never have to look at a clock. On Apple Watch, you'll get a haptic tap at each change, which is how the 10-second sprints work best: eyes up, arms moving. Free accounts can save two custom workouts, enough for a two-block and a three-block version.

Don't add a pace target to the sprints. Ten seconds is too short for GPS pace to settle, and you'll only be chasing a jumpy number. Go by feel.

## Who shouldn't do it

The 10-second sprints are short, but they're fast. If you're in the first couple of months of running, or coming back from an injury, wait until you can run 30 minutes comfortably. Our [Couch to 5K week by week]({% post_url 2026-12-14-couch-to-5k-week-by-week %}) gets you there first.
