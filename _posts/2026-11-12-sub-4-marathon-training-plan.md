Four hours is the marathon's big dividing line. Fewer than half of all marathon finishers get under it, and for most people who do, it marks the moment the marathon stops being something they survived and becomes something they ran.

Like any time goal, it's also just a pace. To run 3:59:59 you need to average **5:41 per kilometre** (9:09 per mile) for 42.2 km. That's the useful part. With a fixed target you don't need a fitness test to set your training paces. Every key session in a sub-4 plan sits a few seconds either side of 5:41.

This guide walks through how a 16-week sub-4 block is built, using the **Sub-4 Marathon** plan in iRunning as the example: who it suits, how the weeks are laid out, and the sessions that matter most. If you're aiming at a spring 2027 marathon, it also tells you which Monday to start.

## Is sub-4 the right goal for you?

Sixteen weeks can take you from "close" to "under". It won't build a marathon from scratch. The plan is a good fit if:

- **Your half marathon is under about 1:55, or your last marathon was 4:05–4:30.** A common rule of thumb is that your marathon time is a little more than double your half time; 1:55 doubles to 3:50, and the extra margin covers the second half of the marathon, which is always harder than the first. If your half is slower than 1:55, the plan suggests the regular **Marathon** plan instead: build the distance this cycle and chase the clock next time.
- **You already run 35 km or more most weeks.** The plan starts at four runs a week and about four hours of running. Jumping into it from 15 km a week is how people get hurt in week 5.
- **You can protect one long run every weekend.** It grows to 2 h 45 m. If your weekends are unpredictable, you can move the long run in the app, but it has to happen somewhere.

If this is your *first* marathon, a time goal is optional. Our [first-marathon guide]({% post_url 2026-09-22-first-marathon-training-plan-16-weeks %}) covers the plan built for finishing, which uses the same 16-week shape with less race-pace work.

## The shape of the 16 weeks

Four runs a week, about 4 h 15 m on a normal week, split into four phases:

| Weeks | Phase | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | **Base** | Threshold tempos from 15 to 20 minutes, long runs from 1:30 to 1:50. Week 4 is a cutback |
| 5–9 | **Build** | Marathon pace appears in week 6 and grows to 30 minutes. First 25K in week 9. Week 8 is a cutback |
| 10–13 | **Peak** | The specific stuff: 35 minutes at pace, long runs that finish at pace, 3 × 5 km. Week 12 is a cutback |
| 14–16 | **Taper** | Volume drops by about a quarter, then a half, then 70%; short touches of pace stay in |

Every normal week has the same four slots: **easy run, quality session, easy run, long run.** The mix works out to about 60% easy running, 30% at marathon pace and 10% threshold and hills.

That 60% is not filler. Most runners who miss sub-4 by a few minutes didn't lack fitness; they ran every easy day at 5:50 and turned up to the marathon-pace sessions too tired to hold the band. If you take one thing from this article: **easy days are slower than you think**, around 6:45–7:00 per km for a sub-4 runner.

## Three paces, all close to 5:41

| Pace | Band per km | Per mile | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Threshold** | 5:20–5:32 | 8:35–8:54 | Base and early Build tempos, 15 to 25 minutes |
| **Marathon pace** | 5:36–5:46 | 9:01–9:17 | Every quality session from week 6, race day |
| **Long-run finish** | 5:38–5:50 | 9:04–9:23 | The last 20–40 minutes of the week 10 and 13 long runs |

Threshold runs a little faster than goal pace. It lifts the ceiling, so 5:41 feels like a cruise rather than a stretch. Marathon pace is the race itself. The long-run finish band is a few seconds kinder, because you'll be running it after two hours on your feet, which is exactly the point.

Easy and long runs have no pace band at all. In iRunning they carry a heart-rate cap instead, so the app tells you when "easy" has drifted into tempo. Our [zone 2 guide]({% post_url 2026-09-06-zone-2-running-aerobic-base %}) explains why that's the better way to keep them honest.

The long run is set in minutes, not kilometres. Two runners on the same plan cover different distances in 2 h 40 m, and that's fine: time on your feet is what builds the endurance. At an easy pace of about 6:15 per km, the longest runs come to roughly 25–27 km.

Why stop at 2 h 45 m and not run 32 km? Past about three hours, a long run costs more recovery than it gives back in fitness, and a 4-hour marathoner running 32 km is out there for well over three hours. The plan gets its specificity from marathon pace *inside* the long run instead of from more kilometres.

## Week by week

| Week | Easy | Quality session | Easy | Long run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 45 min | Tempo 15 min | 38 min | 1:30 |
| 2 | 45 min | Tempo 18 min | 38 min | 1:40 |
| 3 | 45 min | Tempo 20 min | 38 min | 1:50 |
| 4 · cutback | 40 min | Light hills 6 × 45 s | 30 min | 1:15 |
| 5 | 45 min | Tempo 25 min | 38 min | 2:00 |
| 6 | 45 min | Marathon pace 2 × 15 min | 38 min | 2:10 |
| 7 | 45 min | Marathon pace 25 min | 38 min | 2:25 |
| 8 · cutback | 40 min | 30 min easy + 8 strides | 30 min | 1:35 |
| 9 | 45 min | Marathon pace 30 min | 38 min | **2:40 · first 25K** |
| 10 | 45 min | Marathon pace 2 × 20 min | 38 min | 2:30, last 20 at pace |
| 11 | 45 min | Marathon pace 35 min | 38 min | 2:35 |
| 12 · cutback | 40 min | Light fartlek 8 × 1 min | 30 min | 1:45 |
| 13 | 45 min | **Marathon pace 3 × 5 km** | 38 min | **2:45, last 40 at pace** |
| 14 · taper | 40 min | Marathon pace 20 min | 30 min | 1:50 |
| 15 · taper | 35 min | Marathon pace 2 × 8 min | 28 min | 1:15 |
| 16 · race week | 30 min | 20 min easy + 4 strides | 20 min | **Race day** |

Quality sessions start with a 10–12 minute easy warm-up and end with an easy cool-down. Repeated blocks have a 2½-minute easy jog between them; the 5 km reps have 3 minutes.

Four sessions are worth circling in your calendar:

1. **Week 6: marathon pace, 2 × 15 minutes.** Your first taste of race pace, and it should feel almost easy. If it doesn't, your easy days have been too fast.
2. **Week 9: the first 25K.** Slow from the first step, a gel from the first half hour. This is where you find out what your stomach thinks of your fuel.
3. **Week 10: 2:30 with the last 20 minutes at pace.** The first race simulation: race shoes, race breakfast, race gels.
4. **Week 13: the big week.** Three 5 km reps at marathon pace midweek, then the longest run of the plan with its last 40 minutes at pace, close to 28 km in all. Run the reps like the middle of the race, not the end. After Sunday, the taper starts.

## The taper: three weeks, pace kept

The last three weeks cut volume by about 25%, 50% and 70%, but they keep short blocks of marathon pace. That combination, less running but not slower running, is what the research on tapering supports, and it's why you'll arrive at the start feeling springy instead of flat. Feeling sluggish in the middle week is normal. Our [taper guide]({% post_url 2026-09-13-marathon-taper-last-two-weeks %}) goes through it day by day.

## Race day: stay in the band until 30K

The plan's last session is the marathon itself, with the same 5:36–5:46 band you've trained in for ten weeks. The advice is the same as every good marathon plan: **stay inside the band through 30 km, then spend what's left.**

Most failed sub-4 attempts don't fail at 35 km. They fail in the first 10, at 5:25 per km, because it feels easy and the crowd is loud. Going out 15 seconds per kilometre too fast for the first half costs you far more than 15 seconds a kilometre at the end. Our guide to [negative-split pacing]({% post_url 2026-09-08-negative-split-marathon-pacing %}) shows the maths.

3:59:59 is the door. Walk through it evenly.

## How the plan feels in iRunning

- **The band is in your ear.** At the start of each block you hear the target, and if you drift out of 5:36–5:46 you hear *"Pick up the pace"* or *"Slow down"*. On a flat road that's every few minutes at most; you never need to stare at your wrist.
- **Easy days guard themselves.** Easy and long runs carry a zone 2 heart-rate alert. With an Apple Watch or a chest strap, you'll know when "easy" quietly became "tempo".
- **Miles work too.** Switch units to miles and every band is shown per mile: marathon pace becomes 9:01–9:17.
- **Missed a week? Move on, don't start over.** Move a session to another day, pause the plan for an illness, or restart a week. The [missed-run guide]({% post_url 2026-09-11-missed-a-run-pause-move-restart-training-plan %}) shows how.
- **After the race, rest.** When you finish, the app suggests the two-week Post-Race Recovery plan.
