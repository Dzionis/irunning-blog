Nobody dreams of a 40-minute treadmill run. Same view, same belt, a clock that seems to slow down after minute twelve. But the treadmill has two things the road doesn't: perfect control of pace and incline, and zero excuses about weather. Used well, it's the best interval tool you own.

The trick is structure. A belt run with no plan is a hamster wheel. A belt run with eight one-minute surges is a workout that's over before you notice.

## First, switch to Indoor

Before you start a treadmill session in iRunning, tap **Indoor** on the workout screen, right above the Start button. The app remembers the choice for next time.

Here's what Indoor changes:

- **No GPS.** The phone doesn't search for satellites, which saves battery and avoids the "0.00 km" confusion of an outdoor run in a basement gym.
- **Time-based intervals work exactly as outdoors.** Voice cues, countdown beeps and haptics call every change. Put the phone in the cup holder and run by ear.
- **Distance intervals count by time.** With no GPS, a "400 m" step ends after a fixed estimate at a brisk pace, not after 400 m on the belt. So on a treadmill, **build your intervals in minutes, not metres**, and trust the belt's display for distance. The treadmill presets below are all time-based for this reason.
- **Heart rate and cadence still work.** Apple Watch or a chest strap for heart rate; the phone's motion sensors for cadence if it's in a pocket or armband.
- **The run is saved to Apple Health as an indoor run**, so your Fitness app doesn't show a phantom route.

## Three treadmill habits that make intervals work

1. **Change speed early.** A belt takes several seconds to reach the new pace. When the 3·2·1 countdown beeps for a hard interval, start pressing the speed button on the first beep, not after the voice says "Run".
2. **Set 1% incline as your default.** It roughly makes up for the missing wind resistance, so belt effort matches road effort. Save the steep settings for the hills session.
3. **Point a fan at yourself.** Indoors you lose the cooling of moving air, and a hot runner slows down long before a tired one. If the gym has a fan, that's your treadmill.

## A six-week winter plan

If the treadmill is going to be home until spring, the **Winter Treadmill Plan** turns it into a block rather than a series of random sessions. Six weeks, four sessions a week, all indoors, about 2½ hours a week:

- **Easy belt run**, 25–32 minutes at 1% incline. Easy means easy: it's zone 2, and the plan checks your heart rate.
- **Belt intervals**, one a week. It starts at 6 × 1 minute and builds to 6 × 2 minutes in week 3 and 5 × 3 minutes in week 5, the plan's hardest day.
- **Incline climbs**, one a week: 4 to 6 repeats of 1:30–2:00 at a 5–6% incline, with two flat minutes between.
- **Long belt run**, growing from 40 minutes to a full hour in week 6.

Week 4 is a cutback, on purpose. Fitness lands in the lighter weeks.

Prefer to walk? The same Treadmill group has **12-3-30 Incline Walk**, a six-week build-up to the viral 12% incline, 3 mph, 30-minute walk that adds minutes first, then incline, then speed, so calves and lower back arrive ready rather than wrecked.

## Winter isn't wasted

Runners who keep a rhythm through the dark months come out in March with the same engine they had in October, sometimes a better one, because belt intervals are so easy to hit precisely. Pick a preset, tap Indoor, and let the app do the counting.
