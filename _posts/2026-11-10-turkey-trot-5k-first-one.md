More Americans line up to run on Thanksgiving morning than on any other day of the year. Turkey Trots are mostly 5Ks, mostly early, often in costume, and full of people who don't think of themselves as runners: families, friends who signed up after a second glass of wine in October, the uncle who "used to run in college". That makes a Turkey Trot the friendliest first race there is.

This year Thanksgiving is **Thursday, 26 November**, a bit over two weeks from today. That's not enough time to get fit, but it's plenty of time to get ready. Here's how, whether you've never run, run a little, or want to race it.

## Turkey Trots in numbers

- **Over 1.3 million people** registered for a Thanksgiving race on RunSignup alone last year, across more than 1,000 events, 20% more than the year before. The real total is higher, since not every race uses RunSignup.
- **9 in 10 Turkey Trots offer a 5K.** About a quarter add a kids' dash, a fifth a one-mile fun run, and one in eight a 10K.
- **Slightly more women than men run them**, and people in their 30s are the biggest group.
- **The oldest one is in Buffalo**: the YMCA Buffalo Niagara Turkey Trot started in 1896 with six runners, four of whom finished. It's an 8K, and this year is the 131st edition.

A few of the big ones:

Most Turkey Trots are much smaller: the average one has about 1,200 people. Search "turkey trot" plus your town and you'll almost certainly find one within a short drive.

## Can you do it in two weeks?

Yes, as long as you pick the right goal. A 5K is 3.1 miles. At a brisk walk that's about 45–50 minutes, and the big races explicitly welcome walkers. What two weeks *can't* do is turn a non-runner into someone who runs 5K without stopping. Trying is how people end up limping to the turkey.

So pick the plan that matches where you are today:

### 1. You don't run (yet): walk it, with a few jogs

Your goal: finish comfortably, enjoy it, still feel fine at dinner.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| This week, 3 sessions | Walk 30 minutes at a pace where you can still talk. On the third walk, add 6 × 30 seconds of easy jogging, with 2–3 minutes of walking in between |
| Next week, 3 sessions | Walk 35 minutes with 8 × 30 seconds of easy jogging. On one of them, walk the full 5K so you know how long it takes |
| Race week | One easy 20-minute walk on Monday or Tuesday, then rest |

On the day, walk most of it and jog when you feel like it. The run-walk crowd is huge at the back of every Turkey Trot, and nobody is looking at your pace.

### 2. You run a bit: run-walk to the finish

Your goal: get round running most of the way. If you can jog for 10 minutes or more, a 5K is in reach with walk breaks.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| This week, 3 sessions | Run 4 minutes, walk 1 minute, × 5 (25 minutes) |
| Next week, 3 sessions | Run 5 minutes, walk 1 minute, × 5 (30 minutes). On the weekend, try it for the whole 5K distance |
| Race week | One easy 20-minute run-walk early in the week, then rest |

On race day, start with the walk breaks from minute 4, not when you're tired. Planned breaks feel like a choice. Unplanned ones feel like failing, and they come later and last longer.

### 3. You run regularly: race it

Your goal: a time. You already run 5K; two weeks won't add fitness, but they can sharpen what you've got.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| This week | Your usual easy runs, plus one session of 6 × 400 m at your goal 5K pace with 90 seconds of jogging between |
| Next week | Easy runs, plus 3 × 1 km at goal pace with 2 minutes of jogging between |
| Race week | Monday: 20 minutes easy with 4 × 20-second strides. Then easy or nothing until Thursday |

Not sure what goal pace is? Our [guide to good 5K times]({% post_url 2026-10-22-what-is-a-good-5k-time %}) gives typical times by age, and if 30 minutes is the number you're circling, the [sub-30 5K guide]({% post_url 2026-09-12-sub-30-5k-training-plan %}) has the paces.

## What race morning looks like

Turkey Trots are early, cold and crowded. A few things make them easier:

- **Get there 45 minutes early.** Parking and bib pick-up are the hardest part of the morning at the big ones. Many races let you pick up your bib the day before. Do that if you can.
- **Dress for 10 degrees warmer than it is.** Late November mornings are cold, but after the first mile you'll be warm. Wear a layer you can tie around your waist, or an old sweatshirt you don't mind leaving at the start: many races collect them for charity. Gloves and a hat are easy to take off and carry.
- **If you're running in costume, test it first.** A turkey hat that bounces, a cape that tangles your arms, an inflatable anything: run a mile in it at home. Costumes that rub start rubbing at mile two.
- **Line up honestly.** Faster runners at the front, walkers and strollers at the back. Starting too far forward means a mile of being passed, which makes everyone tense. Starting too far back means a mile of weaving, which wastes energy. Big races put pace signs in the corral; use them.
- **Eat what you usually eat.** A light breakfast two hours before, or nothing if the race is very early and you usually run before breakfast. Save the pie.

## How to pace a 5K

Whatever your plan, the first mile decides the race. The start is downhill-fast: adrenaline, a crowd, and people in inflatable costumes sprinting past you. Almost everybody runs the first half-mile too fast.

- **Mile 1: hold back.** It should feel slightly too easy. If you're run-walking, take the first walk break on schedule even though you feel great.
- **Mile 2: settle.** This is the hardest part mentally. You're tired but not close to the end. Pick someone ahead in a funny costume and stay with them.
- **Mile 3 and the last 0.1: go.** If there's anything left, now's the time to spend it.

The [negative-split guide]({% post_url 2026-09-08-negative-split-marathon-pacing %}) is written for marathons, but the idea works at any distance: an even or slightly faster second half almost always beats a fast start and a slow finish.

## Doing it with iRunning

You don't need an app to run a Turkey Trot, but a few free features make the plans above easier:

- **Build the run-walk as a workout.** In the workout builder, add a repeat block of *Run 5 min* and *Walk 1 min* and set it to repeat five times. Voice cues tell you when to switch, so you're not checking your watch at every corner. Free accounts can save two custom workouts, enough for a training session and a race-day version.
- **Pace targets for racers.** Give the work steps a pace range, and you'll hear *"Pick up the pace"* or *"Slow down"* when you drift. In a crowded first mile, that's the brake you need.
- **Run phone-free.** If you have an Apple Watch, the session runs on your wrist with a haptic tap at every change, or send it to Apple's Workout app with **Add to Apple Watch**.
- **Share the finish.** The share cards and run video turn your result into a photo overlay or a short route video for the family group chat, before the turkey's out of the oven.
