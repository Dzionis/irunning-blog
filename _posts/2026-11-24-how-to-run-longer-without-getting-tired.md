You head out, feel fine for five minutes, start breathing hard at eight, and you're walking by ten with your heart pounding and your legs heavy. Next time, the same. It's the most common story in beginner running, and it leads a lot of people to decide they're "not built for it".

They almost always are. The problem is rarely fitness, lungs or genes. It's speed. Here's why you get tired so quickly, and the handful of changes that let you run for 20, 30 or 60 minutes without feeling like you're about to fall over.

## Why you get tired so fast

Your body has two ways to make energy for running. The aerobic system uses oxygen. It's slow to ramp up but can keep going for hours. The anaerobic system doesn't need oxygen, kicks in fast, and runs out in minutes, leaving you breathing hard and with legs that burn.

When you run at a pace that's too fast for your current fitness, you lean on the anaerobic side from the first minute. That's the 8-minute wall. You didn't run out of fitness; you spent it all in the first five minutes.

New runners almost always start too fast, for three reasons:

- **"Running" has a speed in our heads**, and it's usually the speed we ran at school, in a sprint or a race.
- **Slow running feels silly.** If you can walk briskly at 6 km/h, a jog at 7 km/h doesn't feel like running at all. It is.
- **Everyone else looks faster.** The runners you see in the park are the ones who've been doing it for years.

So the first rule of running longer is the least exciting one.

## 1. Slow down. Then slow down a bit more.

The right pace for running longer is the one at which you can **speak in full sentences**. Not sing (that's too easy), not gasp out one word at a time (too hard), but talk. Coaches call this the talk test, and it lines up closely with the easy aerobic zone, which runners call zone 2.

For most new runners, that pace is between 7:30 and 9:00 per kilometre (12–14:30 per mile). For some, it's barely faster than a brisk walk. That's fine. It's still running, and it's the pace at which your heart, lungs and legs adapt fastest without breaking down.

If you have a heart-rate monitor or an Apple Watch, you can make this objective. Our [zone 2 guide]({% post_url 2026-09-06-zone-2-running-aerobic-base %}) explains how to find your zone, and [why your heart rate climbs even when you run slowly]({% post_url 2026-09-25-why-heart-rate-high-running-slow %}) covers the frustrating first weeks when even a slow jog sends it up.

## 2. Let your breathing find its own rhythm

The internet is full of breathing techniques for runners: in through the nose, out through the mouth, three steps in and two out. You don't need any of them to start.

- **Breathe through your mouth and nose together.** At running effort you need more air than your nose alone can deliver. That's normal.
- **Breathe from the belly.** If your shoulders rise with every breath, you're breathing high in the chest. Let your belly push out as you breathe in.
- **If you can't keep a rhythm, you're too fast.** A steady breathing rhythm, something like in for three steps and out for three, appears by itself at the right pace. If you're breathing every step, slow down until it settles.
- **A side stitch** usually means you started too fast or ate too close to the run. Slow to a walk, breathe out hard, and press gently into the sore spot.

Breathing isn't the cause of getting tired. It's the gauge. When it's ragged, the pace is wrong.

## 3. Walk on purpose, not in defeat

Walk breaks aren't cheating. They're the most reliable way to run longer, and they're how almost every beginner programme works, from Couch to 5K to the run-walk method marathoners use.

The trick is to walk **before** you need to. Run 4 minutes, walk 1 minute, repeat. Planned breaks keep you below the point where you'd be forced to stop, so you can do more total running than if you ran until you couldn't. Then, every week or two, stretch the running part and keep the walk the same, until one day the walk isn't needed.

- **If you can run less than 5 minutes:** start with 1 minute running, 1 minute walking, × 10.
- **If you can run 5–10 minutes:** run 4, walk 1, × 5.
- **If you can run 10–15 minutes:** run 8, walk 2, × 2, and build the eights.

## 4. Add time slowly, and in minutes, not kilometres

Bodies adapt in two speeds. Your heart and lungs improve within a few weeks. Tendons, bones and joints take months. That mismatch is why a lot of keen beginners get to 30 minutes in three weeks and then spend a month with sore shins.

The fix is to add very little at a time:

- **Grow your longest run by 2–5 minutes a week**, not 10.
- **Every third or fourth week, go back a step.** Run a little less to let your body catch up. It feels like losing progress; it's how the progress sticks.
- **Count minutes, not kilometres.** If you're tired or it's windy, 20 minutes is still 20 minutes, even if it's 2.4 km instead of 2.8. You'll never have to rush to "finish the distance".

## 5. Three times a week beats once

Running twice a week builds fitness slowly. Running three times a week, with a rest day between, is where it starts to show. A single heroic long run on Sunday after six days off is the worst of both: you're too unpractised to enjoy it, and you're sore for days. Short and often wins.

## 6. The small things that add up

- **Eat something, but not too close.** A light snack 1–2 hours before a run helps; a big meal 30 minutes before guarantees a stitch.
- **Drink normally during the day.** For runs under an hour, you don't need to carry water.
- **Sleep.** Running on five hours of sleep feels twice as hard, because it is.
- **Run somewhere flat.** Hills are great later. When you're trying to run longer, they push you over the line on every climb.
- **Look up.** Head up, shoulders down and loose, arms relaxed at about 90 degrees. Tension costs energy you'll want at minute 20.

| Week | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 min | 8 + 8 min, 2 min walk between | 12 min |
| 2 | 12 min | 10 + 10 min, 2 min walk | 15 min |
| 3 | 15 min | 12 + 12 min, 90 s walk | **20 min** |
| 4 · cutback | 12 min | 10 min | 15 min |
| 5 | 20 min | 15 + 10 min, 90 s walk | 24 min |
| 6 | 25 min | 20 min | **30 min nonstop** |

Every run starts and ends with five minutes of walking. Notice what's *not* in the table: no distances, no paces, no "faster than last week". The only target is time on your feet at a pace where you could talk.

Can't run 10 minutes yet? Start with run-walk intervals (section 3), or with a plan that builds the first ten minutes, and come back to this one. Already at 15–20 minutes? Start from week 3.
